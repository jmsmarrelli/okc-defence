Method notes

What the two channels are, why the attempt differential is allowed to stand in for a turnover, and how much of any of it survives to next season.

The decomposition

Every attempt a team faced is labeled with one of five zones. Against that season's league-average points per attempt in each zone, the points a team allowed per shot split in two:

points allowed per shot  -  league average
    = (what their opponents' locations were worth at league rates)   <- location
    + (what their opponents actually scored, minus that)             <- conversion

Multiplied by 100 it reads as points per hundred attempts faced. Negative is good defense in both terms and in their sum, throughout.

League rates are re-estimated every season. A corner three was worth 1.16 points in 2024-25 and 1.09 in 2004-05, and a decomposition run against a pooled rate would price two decades of rule changes and shot-selection change as defense.

Five zones, not coordinates. The raw files have two defects that make finer descriptions unusable across the sample: the action-type vocabulary was rewritten between 2007 and 2009, and the shot coordinates were recorded differently before 2010-11, with three season files published on a different scale entirely. Both are documented at length in the sibling nba-shot-value repository. The five BASIC_ZONE labels have meant the same thing throughout, so they are all this project uses.

The attempt differential

This is the load-bearing piece and it deserves its own scrutiny.

A steal writes no row into a shot file. What it writes is the absence of one. Both teams in a game see the same possessions to within one, so

(own attempts per game) - (opponent attempts per game)

is the shortfall a defense created: possessions the opponent never got to shoot on, plus possessions the defense got twice. It mixes turnovers forced with the offensive rebounding battle, and nothing here separates them.

Converting it to points uses that season's league-average points per attempt: a denied attempt is worth what an average attempt was worth. That is the right order of magnitude and it is not exact, because the attempts a defense denies are not a random sample of attempts.

What it is not. It is not a turnover rate. A team with a poor offensive rebounding rate of its own will show a worse attempt differential than its defense deserves, and this project makes no attempt to correct for that, because correcting it needs rebounding data the file does not carry.

Why points per game rather than per possession

Because there is no possession count in a shot file, and inventing one from attempts would be circular in a project whose second channel is attempts.

The cost is real: a fast team looks like it denies more points a game than the same team playing slow, so the leaderboard has a mild pace tilt. It is a tilt and not a confound — the league's pace range across a season is roughly ten per cent — and the alternative was worse.

Stability, and what it licenses

Every component is run against the same team's next season, across the 624 consecutive team-season pairs in the sample. Opponent shooting percentages are first expressed against that season's league average:

Component Repeats at
Shot locations forced 0.66
Opponent two-point percentage 0.58
Points denied per game 0.51
Conversion suppressed 0.51
Attempt differential 0.44
Opponent three-point percentage 0.19

The centering matters. League two-point percentage climbed from .460 to .545 over these twenty-two seasons, and a raw year-to-year correlation picks that climb up as if every team were repeating itself: uncentred, opponent two-point percentage "repeats" at 0.85. Measured against the league it is 0.58. The three-point side barely moves either way (0.16 raw, 0.19 centered), because league three-point accuracy hardly changed.

The last row is the one that matters and it is not news to anyone who has looked at defensive statistics before; it is here because it changes what can be said about this particular season. Splitting the conversion channel by whether the shot was worth three puts −2.81 of Oklahoma City's −5.37 on the two-point side and −2.57 on the three-point side, and the second of those numbers is a property of the season more than of the team.

This is a bound, not an identification. It says how much of a typical team's conversion edge carries forward. It cannot say which of Oklahoma City's individual possessions were contest quality and which were a shooter missing an open look.

Why "points denied" rather than a rating

The composite adds the two channels and reports one number in points a game. It is deliberately not called a defensive rating, because it is not one: no free throws, no possessions, no pace adjustment.

What it has instead is that it is built from what a defense did — the locations it forced, the conversion it allowed, the attempts it denied — rather than from what its opponents happened to score. Those are different objects and the difference is the point of building it.

Where this is weakest

Fouling is invisible. A defense that concedes free throws instead of shots looks better here than it is, and the effect is not small: free throws are about a fifth of scoring. Any team whose shot defense looks unusually good should be checked against its free-throw rate allowed before anyone believes it. That check cannot be run from this file.

The subject is one season. Twenty-two seasons of context around a single team-season is the right shape for the question "how unusual was that", and the wrong shape for "how good is this team". The season-by-season table is there so the reader can see how much of it is new.

Opponent quality is not controlled. A defense that played a soft schedule looks better here. Over 82 games the schedule mostly evens out, and this is a real limitation on any single-season comparison — particularly between conferences, which do not play each other equally.

Regular season only, 2003-04 to 2024-25, which is where the shot mirror currently ends. Nothing in this repository is about a playoff series, and a seven-game series against one opponent is not the thing an 82-game average predicts well.
